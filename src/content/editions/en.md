# How We Forget to Fly

## A move of our own

We begin by following an example. We watch someone hold a pencil, fold a plane, solve a problem. Other people's experience gives us a place to start. Then a question of our own appears: why does the plane keep turning to one side?

We check the instructions, straighten a wing, launch it again. Look at what changed. The paper plane becomes our problem. We begin to learn things about it that we had never noticed.

As we grow older, we collect ready answers. Experience sometimes brings a habit of skipping the question: this has been done, this is obvious, this is how it is supposed to work. We know the familiar road and notice less often where it no longer fits.

We can forget Flight while keeping everything we know about wings.

AI now helps us write, draw, program and compare possibilities. With its help, an idea once difficult even to show can become a first draft. It invites us to take on something for which we previously lacked the tools or skill. This is where creative thinking becomes especially useful: seeing the task, choosing what matters and understanding what to do with the result.

Suppose we want to create a useful product. Before asking for a hundred ideas, it is worth noticing one human difficulty. Then understanding why familiar solutions fail to resolve it. And making a trial that lets us learn something new. There is work for AI at each of these turns. The direction becomes clearer as we learn to distinguish the task ourselves.

This book is about that way of thinking and acting. About moving beyond a ready answer, finding a move of our own and turning it into an object, a text, a device or a change in everyday life. About learning to do this ourselves and giving children the experience of making their own discoveries.

Creative freedom becomes tangible when there is room to try something our own way. Flight needs energy, attention and work through which we are willing to find something out.

Let us begin where the familiar map no longer explains what is in front of us.

## Manifesto: the right to your own question

### 1. Everyone has the right to try

You need no permission to call yourself creative before starting a drawing, inventing a device or changing a familiar way of working. The work will reveal what we can do and what we still need to learn. A successful piece brings pleasure; a mistake needs examining. Neither determines a person's worth.

### 2. A question of your own deserves attention

It may arise inside someone else's assignment: why are we doing it this way? Whom should it help? What remains unclear? Authorship begins with taking part in finding the task's meaning. Sometimes that participation changes everything that follows; sometimes it changes one small but significant detail.

### 3. Experience helps us see further

We use other people's knowledge, tools and discoveries. A good Map shows where people have been and what they noticed. It has a scale and a purpose. When the terrain changes, we should check which lines still help and which need drawing again.

### 4. Thought develops through making

An idea needs to meet something that can answer: a material, a person, a circumstance. At first, we may give shape to just one hunch. A sketch, model, conversation or small trial will bring details that were missing from our first impression.

### 5. Flight rests on Ground

Work has conditions: a body, time, a place, skill, available help. We can arrange and change them. A quiet hour, a learned technique or a cleared table sometimes opens more possibilities than a demand to try harder. Rest belongs to life even on days when no idea appears.

### 6. Freedom needs a Compass

Our intentions meet other people's lives. We need to understand whom they affect, what has been agreed and what we are responsible for. Within these conditions, room for choice remains. We can revise our course when a fact we did not know appears, or a voice we had not heard.

### 7. Children need the experience of their own discovery

An adult can show a technique, help with a difficult part and make the work safe. But between question and answer there should be room for the child to try, notice and decide. Working together lets us see how another person thinks, including their mistakes and changes of mind.

### 8. You can begin within the life you have

Work, care, chores and interests already contain material for new questions. A beginning can fit into one evening. The size of a first attempt depends on what we want to find out and the energy available now. The larger intention will become clearer afterwards.

### 9. The Wind can be given a job

AI helps us look for alternatives, notice weaknesses, learn tools and give a hunch a shape. We can adopt its proposals, change them and develop an unexpected turn. When choosing a result for use, we need to understand what it does and what still needs checking.

### 10. What we make needs a Return

After its first encounter, the work changes in our eyes. Someone used it differently. A material behaved unexpectedly. An error or a new use appeared. Attention to this response turns a single attempt into experience we can build on. Harm done calls for repair.

### 11. A path has turns and stopping places

Not every idea deserves to continue. We may notice that the question is exhausted, conditions have changed or help is needed. Ending is also a decision: return what was borrowed, keep promises, leave a clear place from which to resume. Life continues beyond this work.

### 12. This map can be redrawn

Call, Compass, Lift, Making, Flight, Return, Ground: words through which we examine our own experience. They are useful while they help us see something more clearly. Readers will keep their own words, questions and ways of making. The book ends before its readers' possibilities do.

## Where a new move comes from

A task often begins with a small mismatch. A shelf looks right in the sketch but stops the door opening. An explanation is clear to its writer, while someone else stumbles at the second sentence. An app offers more and more options, and choosing becomes harder.

Stay with that spot for a moment. What did we expect to see? What happened? What assumption were we relying on?

A question appears between expectation and result. In this book's language, that is a Call. It may be very quiet: “Why are we doing it this way at all?”

### The energy to notice

Sometimes the question has long been in front of us, yet we keep repeating a familiar action. We have moved the button, changed the colour, rewritten the heading, and still have not looked at what someone is trying to do on that screen.

In that state, it helps to examine the work itself: is there time to look at the task calmly, is it too large, what distracts us, what is missing for the next attempt? After hours of fruitless variation, another option may offer less than a pause and a fresh look.

Here, we will use energy potential to mean the reserve of energy and readiness to engage in the work. It is a working concept for observing ourselves. In one situation we lack rest; in another, a clear starting point; in a third, interest in a task we took on out of habit.

Try reducing the scale: today, watch someone go through one screen; read one paragraph aloud; assemble one joint. That attempt will help show where to put effort. Sometimes it draws us further in. Sometimes it helps us notice that it is time to stop for today.

This is Ground: the real conditions that support an idea. Lift begins when the next action has something to rest on.

### Thought acquires hands

In our heads, the shelf fits, the sentence sounds right, the mechanism works. To see the details, we have to give the idea a shape.

Fold a sheet. Draw a screen. Explain it to someone. Cut a full-size shelf from cardboard and hold it against the wall.

Thought receives an answer from the material. The cardboard hits the door. An unnecessary step appears in the drawing. We hear a promise in the sentence that we never intended to make. Now we have an observation to work from.

We will call this connection between thought, movement and perception psychomotor activity. We refine an idea by acting, and change the action according to what we notice. A learned movement helps us think, too: once the hand knows how to make a fold, we can investigate what shape to build with it.

Imagine a paper bridge between two cups. The sheet bends under a toy car. The first thought is to use thicker paper. But we can change the shape: fold the same sheet like an accordion and put it back on the cups. Comparing the attempts reveals a connection between the folds and the load the bridge carries. The next question is more precise: which folds help, and which only make it harder for the car to cross?

Such a trial creates information that is new to us. Before it, we could list guesses. Afterwards, we know what happened under particular conditions and can set up the next experiment.

### A measure of our own

Being surprised by a result is not enough. We need to understand exactly what worked.

By critical judgement, we mean the ability to notice mismatches and judge a solution by the substance of the task. If someone is looking for a button, what matters is whether they find it. The beauty of the drawing and our pleasure in making it do not answer that question.

Before changing the solution, finish this sentence: “I will consider this attempt useful if…” Choose a sign you can observe. The explanation will let someone perform the action without another hint. The bridge will hold the car. The new shelf will let the door open.

A first trial may have a more modest aim: to find where the bridge bends most. Even a falling car will then provide a useful answer. The search has moved forward, though a working bridge still needs making.

This sign helps us choose between alternatives. It may also need revising. If the door opens but nobody can reach the shelf, the task was broader than we thought. Our own measure becomes more precise through encounters with other people's experience and real conditions. This is how the Compass works.

Energy lets us enter the search. Action gives thought material. Critical judgement helps us see what happened and where to go. In the work, they keep supporting one another.

### When the question itself changes

Sometimes we spend a long time adjusting details before noticing the assumption that tied our hands. We kept looking for thicker paper. It turned out we could change the shape of the sheet.

That turn can feel like a leap: a second ago, no other way was visible; now it seems obvious. With the bridge, attention to the bend, a familiar fold, an attempt and a comparison lie behind the turn. Flight finds its direction within this work.

A new move may well connect things already known. What matters is the connection we discover for our task. Other people's experience helps us look for it: a familiar technique may have a use we had not seen before.

Choose one small difficulty in something you are doing now. Write down the familiar solution and the assumption holding it up. Find a way to test that assumption so you can see the result. After the trial, leave two lines: what you expected and what happened. The difference will suggest the next question.

This is Return. What we made brings experience back to us, and we begin the next attempt with it.

## What is for dinner?

*An imagined story about developing a product. It shows a way of working; the observations and results described belong to this example.*

You have an idea for an app that helps people decide what to cook. The first version is already visible: recipes, photographs, filters, recommendations. Ask AI to suggest features, and the list grows longer.

But it is still unclear when the person needs help.

### At the fridge

You ask someone you know to show you how they usually choose dinner. In the evening, they open the fridge, then their saved recipes. One recipe needs several ingredients they do not have. Another takes too long. A third requires remembering what is already cooked and what remains from last night's dinner.

They have recipes. You notice a different difficulty: each choice has to be reconciled afresh with shopping, leftover ingredients and time.

Three separate lines appear in your notes:

- **Observation:** the person switched several times between the fridge, recipes and shopping list.
- **Assumption:** they find it difficult to bring this information together into one decision.
- **Question:** could we help them make that connection?

For now, this is an assumption about one case. Watch a few more ordinary evenings and talk to people with different habits. The cause may turn out to be something else. But it is already clear that adding recipes, by itself, tests nothing.

### The first attempt fits on a sheet

Together, you make a plan for several dinners and one shopping list. No app: a sheet on the fridge lets you try the idea itself. Agree in advance to discuss when the plan helped, where it had to change and what required an extra decision.

You can ask AI for several ways of grouping dinners around shared shopping. You review the suggestions together, check them against the person's usual meals and choose what they are willing to try. Now the Wind has a specific job, and its result has a clear test.

The plan helped on the first evening. On the next, the person ran late and ate out, so the cooking shifted. After a few days, the writing on the sheet no longer matched what was in the fridge.

The first solution was too rigid.

We could remind the person to follow the schedule. We could add notifications. But that would keep defending our assumption: dinners must be assigned to days.

Ask it differently: why did we assign them to days in the first place?

### A plan that can move

The task changes. Now we are looking for a way to keep the benefit of shared shopping when an evening goes differently from expected.

Separate cards are enough for the second trial. Each holds one dinner, its ingredients and approximate cooking time. There is no date. Cooked meals are put aside; the remaining cards can be rearranged.

For now, everything on the cards is handwritten. After one dinner, some ingredients have been used, while the other cards still show the old set. The fridge needs checking again.

Now the digital product has a more precise task: after a chosen dinner, update the remaining possibilities to reflect what was used. Earlier, we planned another recipe showcase. In this version, the main screen helps with the next dinner while taking previous decisions into account.

This is the new move in this story. We can trace its source: watching at the fridge, an overly rigid plan, a missed evening, rearrangeable cards, the discovery that remaining ingredients need updating.

### What to give the Wind

Now AI can sketch that screen or help assemble a prototype. Describe a particular situation:

> Dinner was moved. Some ingredients have already been used in another dish. Suggest three ways to show what can still be cooked. For each, say what information the person must enter and where they might become confused.

The answer gives alternatives to consider. One may require too much manual entry; another may hide needed information; a third may suggest a useful detail. From them, we still have to assemble the next trial.

The critic's role helps here too: ask for the assumptions supporting the chosen option. Are people willing to record every use of an ingredient? Would a rough estimate of what remains be enough? These questions then need checking with people. AI's imagined reactions help us prepare for the encounter.

A similar product may already exist. Find and study it: which solutions work there, where must the user adapt, what can we learn without another invention? It is another map for our search.

### What came back with us

We have no basis yet for calling this a successful product. We have a more precise task and a few details that have been tested. The next version may also be awkward: tracking ingredients might take more effort than it saves. We would then need to simplify the idea or look for another way to help.

But now we know what to test. Each change rests on an observation, rather than a general wish to make the app more interesting.

In your own work, you can begin with an equally small spot. Watch how someone handles a difficulty now. Separate what you saw from your guess. Give that guess a usable form. Stay close enough, long enough, to notice what the attempt taught you.

A map has returned to the table with corrections. Some lines have been erased. We are drawing one road for the first time.

## Begin within the life you have

A question may be waiting inside an ordinary task that repeats every week. For a first attempt, it can be enough to pause where you have long been acting out of habit.

Imagine that you keep explaining one procedure to colleagues. There is already an instruction sheet; everything is written down. Yet people return with questions, and you answer in almost the same words. It begins to irritate you.

You could add to the instructions again. Or ask someone to perform the action in front of you and watch where they stop. They reach a choice between two routes. Both are described correctly. What is unclear is which applies to their case.

Now a small question of your own appears: how could we help with this particular choice? The first trial might be a drawing with two branches and one clarifying question. Afterwards, you will see whether the branches help or whether you misunderstood why the person stopped.

### Where you stopped looking

Experience lets us act without lengthy deliberation. That is convenient while conditions are familiar. Sometimes the same experience hides a change: we already know why someone did not understand, an object broke or a conversation never happened.

There is a useful check for this knowledge: can you point to the observation behind it? “They do not read carefully” is an explanation. “They stopped in front of two options” is something we can see. The second leaves more paths open for investigation.

Choose a recurring inconvenience and watch it once without the familiar explanation. Write down the sequence of events. It may confirm what you already knew. It may reveal a missed detail.

Gravity often lives here: the answer arrives so quickly that we stop checking whether it applies to what is happening.

### If the idea stays in the notebook

Sometimes the question exists, but beginning is still hard. A title, a diagram and several possibilities fill the notebook. Each time you open it, you add another detail. The idea itself becomes harder to try.

Choose one question you need to answer before further planning will help. Will someone understand the drawing? Will the structure fit the recess? Can this text be spoken aloud without explanation? Make a version just for that question. The unfinished parts can stay in the notebook for now.

If a particular skill is missing, name it. “I cannot make apps” may conceal a small need: linking two drawn pages with a transition. That operation can be learned, demonstrated by someone else or given to a helper. Decide beforehand what you want to learn yourself.

### Finding a voice

The attempt may be artistic. Imagine the opening of a story: you have described the character, their past and personality in detail, yet the scene still feels motionless. Try removing the explanation and giving the character one action. Suppose they have come to collect an old possession and, for some reason, linger at the door.

Write that moment. A movement of the hand or an ill-timed remark may suggest something about the character you did not yet know. Read both versions aloud: where did you become interested, and which detail held you? Later, you could ask a reader to tell you what stayed with them.

Here, the measure is an artistic intention. It too grows clearer in the work: you meant to write about leaving, but the scene began speaking about reluctance to part. Examine that turn before restoring everything to the original plan.

### Continuing without losing the thread

An attempt rarely ends at the exact moment the question does. You need to collect a child, cook dinner or return to other work. Leave your future self a short note: what is known, what is still only assumed and which action to resume with.

The next day, it will return you to the task without having to untangle the whole idea again. A failed sample may lie beside it. A preserved mistake sometimes says more than a neatly rewritten plan.

There is another possible ending: after a pause, the question no longer attracts you. Understand what changed, close your obligations and make room. The right to stop lets us choose work in which something remains to be discovered.

Flight does not require the rest of life to wait aside. It can begin with a single question mark in the margin of an ordinary day.

## AI as Wind

The Wind can tempt us: it brings a great deal ready-made. A few paragraphs, pictures, plans, and we already want to pick the best. Meanwhile, the original question may still be unclear.

We can arrange work with AI around what needs finding out now. Sometimes we need another view of the task. Sometimes a form we can show. Sometimes help with an operation our hands have not yet learned. The same tool can help in different ways.

Take an imagined task: you are preparing an announcement for a neighbourhood workshop. People who know nothing of your intention need to understand it.

### Mirror: seeing your own draft

Your notes contain everything: materials, the workshop's history, your reasons for organising it, the meeting time. Ask AI to describe what it understood from the sketch and name separately what remains unclear.

If its answer turns the workshop into a session for experienced makers while you expect beginners, find the phrase that created this impression. It may come from your text. The system may have filled in missing information. Either way, there is now a spot to examine.

A mirror helps us see a possible reading. It does not know our intention better than we do; sometimes the mismatch is the most useful part of its answer.

### Conversation partner: finding the missing question

Ask for questions whose answers are needed to understand the invitation. Should people bring tools? Can they come with no experience? How do they find the entrance?

Choose what applies to your meeting. Answers about time, place and conditions come from the organisers. The rest can be marked unresolved for now. That alone separates an expressive sentence from information the announcement still lacks.

### Generator: moving in different directions

Now you need approaches to presentation. If you ask for ten announcements without further conditions, they may differ mainly in wording. It is more useful to name the approaches you want to compare: a brief invitation, a captioned drawing, an account of what happens in one evening.

Look at what each makes visible and what it loses. An unexpected detail in the answer may suggest a new way of describing the meeting. Develop it if it helps the intention. Choose according to what a future participant will understand and whether they can decide if the invitation suits them.

### Craft assistant: making a trial possible

You have chosen a drawing with short captions but cannot yet assemble the layout. AI can help with the image, composition or page code. Give it verified information and describe what the reader needs to see first. Check that the draft has kept those details and has not added promises you never made.

If you want to learn the technique itself, ask for one operation to be explained and repeat it with your own hands. If trying the announcement matters more right now, accept wider help. These are two different intentions; distinguish them before beginning.

### Critic: setting a precise measure

Instead of a general judgement of whether it turned out well, ask about a specific risk: what in the layout prevents a beginner from understanding that they are welcome too? Which words might read as mandatory requirements? Where did the date get lost?

A comment should have a basis that can be checked in the text or image. A preference for a livelier tone can remain an opinion. A missing address needs correcting. Distinguishing them keeps your own judgement of the work available.

### Simulator: preparing for the encounter

Ask how someone who wants to come but has never held a tool might read the announcement. Which sentence might stop them? What would they need to find out separately? Such a rehearsal suggests places to check. Imagined reactions do not yet tell us how the neighbours will read it.

With their consent, show the layout to someone it is actually addressed to. Ask what will happen at the meeting and what they need to do to attend. Watch where they look for that information. Their answer may surprise both you and the list of possible reactions the system supplied.

### Returning to what you wanted to make

If there are too many options, set the original note beside them: whom you invite, what will happen, what the person needs to know. It will help you choose what matters again.

Participants' personal details are usually unnecessary for this work. Use invented data for the draft, then add the verified public address and time when preparing the announcement. Before sharing it, check the content and agree on anything affecting the other organisers.

Part of the idea may have arisen in conversation with AI. You may have kept another part against its advice. Authorship also appears in your ability to explain that choice, see its consequences and return to the work when it needs repair.

The Wind helped lift the sheet. Now we need to see where it reaches.

## Growing a habit of discovery

A child meets a world where much has already been done for them. Adults know how to hold a spoon, fold clothes and cross the road. The child learns from them and gains necessary support. Alongside this, there are tasks whose answers they can still seek for themselves.

In such work, it helps to give a child the experience of the whole movement: wanting to make something, trying, seeing the result, changing the solution. Repeated attempts can grow into a habit of inquiry. Adults support it through the way they arrange the shared work.

### The car cannot turn

Imagine a child building a town from boxes. They have made a tunnel, but the toy car gets stuck at the turn. You think the passage needs widening. Your hand is already reaching to adjust the wall.

Before changing the structure, you can suggest looking together at where the car touches it. The child moves the car slowly and notices a mark on the cardboard. Now you share a point of attention.

“What would you like to try?” They decide to widen the entrance. You help with cutting if it requires a tool an adult still needs to handle. The car goes further but still cannot turn.

You could enlarge the opening again. Or remove the roof and watch from above. In this story, the child notices that an inside corner is in the way and moves one wall. The car passes through.

The work may end there. Another may begin: now the bus does not fit the tunnel. An interesting question often comes from the play itself.

### Show a technique and give the work back

Independence is compatible with learning. You can show how to draw a line with a ruler, make a strong fold or join two pieces. It helps if the child understands which difficulty the technique resolves and can immediately use it in their own construction.

If an adult takes on a dangerous or overly difficult operation, name its boundary: “I will make this cut. You show me where the wall should be.” Help then remains part of the shared intention, while the child keeps a decision they can make themselves.

Some questions have no answer even for the adult. You can say, “I don't know if it will hold. How shall we find out?” In a shared trial, the child sees that not knowing is compatible with knowing how to act.

### What to notice in the result

A conversation about the finished thing can begin with what happened: “At first the car got stuck here. You moved the wall, and it went through.” The sentence makes the connection between decision and result visible.

Then ask what the child noticed. They may care more about the tunnel's colour or the driver's story. Shared work can mean different things to its participants. Not every discovery needs an immediate lesson.

If the child is upset, first help them handle what happened. Later, return to the object: where it tore, what held, which part they still want to try. Judging a structure is easier when the conversation does not become a judgement of the child.

### Conditions we can arrange

Such attempts need available materials, a place and time that allows for remaking. If every box has to be begged for and the table must be cleared in five minutes, scale the idea to those conditions beforehand.

An adult can name a simple frame: which materials are available, what help there is, when things need putting away. Real decisions remain for the child inside it. If the passage to the door must stay clear, call that a condition of shared space and find another place for the town together.

Adults have limits too. “Today I can only help with the cutting” offers clearer support than agreeing to a large undertaking without the energy to see it through. The construction can wait until tomorrow.

### When AI is nearby

Suppose the child wants to invent inhabitants for the town. You could ask AI for several creatures with different habits. The child then chooses, changes, draws their own character and tries imagining how it lives in the tunnel they built. The system's answer becomes material for play.

If the aim was to learn to invent independently, the adult can limit the help to one question or detail. If the interest lies in the town's structure just now, a ready-made character may be enough to begin. Understand which action remains the child's and why the tool is needed.

The adult is responsible for choosing an available, age-appropriate way to work with AI and for personal information. The game does not require giving the system the child's name, photographs or conversations at home. And the first person to talk to about the invented character is its author.

The town has a new road. The child is already carrying something of their own along it.

## Atlas: where to begin the next attempt

Sometimes reading leaves a question of your own. Here are small ways to work with it. Choose what fits your situation. One attempt is enough to give you material for the next decision.

### 1. Find support

Take an idea you keep putting off. Name the next action and what you need in order to take it: a clear surface, a tool, a demonstration of one technique, someone's help. Try arranging one of these supports. Then see whether beginning has changed. If another person is needed, discuss help they can reasonably give. The concrete Ground beneath the idea becomes visible.

### 2. Catch the question before the answer

Write down a familiar inconvenience without explaining its cause. For example: “After completing the form, the person returns to the beginning and looks for their entry.” Beside it, list possible explanations, leaving them as guesses. Choose one and consider what observation could test it. “Where does the person expect to see the entry?” leads to a different trial from an already chosen solution: “We need a brighter button.”

### 3. Leave a real choice

Recall an invitation to do something together. What has already been decided, and what can the other person actually change? Name both parts. For a family construction, these might be the time to tidy up and freedom to choose the structure itself. If the child chose the subject, ask what help they need. During the next attempt, look for a decision you did not suggest yourself.

### 4. Change one assumption

Choose a familiar solution and find a rule you accepted without checking. “Dinner belongs to a particular day” in our story is such a rule. Imagine a version without it. First consider the useful work the rule did; then try keeping that benefit another way. A sketch will show whether a new direction has appeared or the old constraint remains necessary for now.

### 5. Give the Wind one task

Choose an action AI can help with now: reflect a draft, suggest different approaches, assemble a trial or find a weak assumption. Frame the request so it is clear what you want and how you will examine it. After the answer, note one decision you adopted and explain to yourself why. If there is no explanation, return to the original question.

### 6. Keep the surprise

After a trial, write two short lines: “I expected…” and “What happened was…” Add a detail worth checking again. It may be something someone said, a mark on the material or the moment an action changed. The note is first of all for you, so that tomorrow you do not replace what happened with a tidier story. If the result recurs, it will be easier to compare with the first attempt.

### 7. Change the place for working

Look at what you have to do before beginning: find scissors, ask for a box, move a lamp, clear the table. Choose one inconvenience and change the arrangement. For children's materials, consider what can safely remain accessible without an adult. After a while, see whether the new arrangement helps or gets in the way of shared life. Creative conditions can also be tested through action.

### 8. Hear the answer

Choose something you made and a question about it that remains open. If the answer depends on another person, agree on a small trial. Ask them to perform the relevant action and watch without immediately offering hints. Afterwards, discuss the point that caused difficulty. Record what you saw separately from your explanation: they may diverge. Now you can decide which change to try next.

### 9. End in a way that allows a return

Put away materials, return borrowed things and name any remaining obligation. If the work continues, leave the next small operation somewhere visible. If it is finished, note what you learned and can take into other work. An unanswered question may remain unanswered. A clear stopping place frees attention for what waits beyond the edge of the table.

### 10. Read someone else's map

Find an existing solution to a similar task: an object, text, instruction or product. Try to understand whom it works for and under what conditions. Choose one technique useful to you. Separately, note a condition that differs in your situation. Now you can borrow deliberately, understanding which part to keep and which you will need to test or change.

## The Sky Remains Open

Two versions of one thing lie on the table. The first shows where you began. The second shows what you managed to notice. Between them remain a conversation, a failed attempt, someone else's hint and a turn that was not in the plan.

Soon the table will need clearing. Some sheets will go into a box; something will be finished, something will wait for another day. The question has already changed your view: a detail has appeared in a familiar thing that you used to pass by.

This experience is invisible from outside. It will stay with you when you next need to think something up. Perhaps in entirely different work.

Outside the window, the wind lifts the edge of a sheet forgotten on the sill. It has no folds yet.

The page ends here. The Sky does not.
